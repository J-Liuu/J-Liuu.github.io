01

The challenge

Make a complex system visible without making the interface overwhelming.

Cell transport is dynamic, microscopic, and difficult to understand through diagrams alone. An interactive simulation can make the system tangible, but only if students understand what to do, where to look, and why the outcome matters.

Our product challenge was to balance scientific meaning, playful interaction, and classroom clarity for grades 6–8.

02

Research and discovery

Classroom voices shaped the product requirements.

I helped co-lead weekly conversations with teachers from multiple school districts and helped organize student think-aloud testing. The research was qualitative: we listened for confusion, hesitation, and places where the interface competed with the learning goal.

03

Key product decisions

A repeatable learning loop keeps attention on cause and effect.

1Preview

Introduce one scientific rule.

2Practice

Show the exact interaction.

3Observe

Make the consequence visible.

4Reflect

Connect evidence to the concept.

Decision 01

Progressive disclosure over a crowded control panel

Each lesson isolates a single membrane behavior before asking students to combine them. This lowers the number of rules and controls a student must hold in memory at once.

  1. 1A Oxygen passes through
  2. 1B Large molecules bounce
  3. 1C Water uses a channel
  4. 1D Glucose uses a door
  5. 1E Waste exits in vesicles
  6. 1F Combine all three paths
Decision 02

Use interaction behavior as the explanation

Instead of relying on text alone, the simulation makes each rule behave differently. A correct path crosses the membrane; an incorrect path visibly resists the student's action.

Decision 03

Turn feedback into instructional evidence

Progress counts answer “Did it work?” while animation and recap text answer “Why?” Hints repeat the goal, scenario, interaction, and evidence without requiring a separate assistant.

Decision 04

Keep AI support optional, not foundational

The build includes an experimental CytoBot path with local fallback guidance. Core instructions, evidence, and recaps remain available without an API connection, so the learning flow does not depend on AI access.

Design principleStudents should be able to learn from the system itself.

04

Outcome

A testable learning experience, not a claim of proven impact.

What we built

A browser-based sequence of six membrane transport lessons with onboarding, direct manipulation, contextual hints, visible progress, and concept recaps.

What I contributed

Interaction and UI design, translating science requirements into mechanics, iterative prototyping, educator sessions, and student usability-test planning.

What remains open

The materials document a qualitative research process, not measured learning gains, adoption, or effectiveness. Those require further classroom validation.

Reflection

Design the guidance as carefully as the interaction.

This project reinforced that educational products cannot treat instructions as a layer added after the experience. The preview, gesture, feedback, and recap are all part of the same interaction model.

What I would test next

  • Whether students can state the objective before beginning each lesson.
  • Which feedback states students notice without prompting.
  • How keyboard, motion, and contrast options affect access.
  • What teachers need to fit the activity into a real lesson plan.
